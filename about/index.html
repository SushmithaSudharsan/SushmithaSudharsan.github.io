ABOUT

I have always been curious about what makes things work.

Even before I knew the word debugging, I liked figuring out why something wasn't behaving the way I expected. I would try something, change it, break it, and try again just to understand what was happening underneath.

That curiosity eventually led me to programming and then machine learning. I liked that ML turned the same curiosity into bigger questions: Is the data right? Why did the result change? What happens when the inputs change? And how do you make something work beyond a notebook?

My first deeper experience with that came through undergraduate research, where I explored classroom engagement and fatigue using an RNN/LSTM model. The work became my first-author IEEE publication, but what stayed with me most was learning how to find meaningful signal in real data.

Then came one of the biggest firsts in my life: moving to Boston for my MS in Data Analytics Engineering at Northeastern University. It was my first time living away from home and figuring out a completely new place on my own.

My first Boston winter is still one of my favorite memories. Seeing snow for the first time was exciting, and learning to actually live in that cold was an experience of its own. By my second winter, I was casually walking through the snow on the way to class.

Living independently taught me much more than I expected. I learned to cook, manage finances and chores, coordinate with housemates, navigate public transportation, and balance everyday responsibilities with university. Living and working with people from different backgrounds also taught me to communicate, adapt, and understand different ways of doing things.

Northeastern also gave me the opportunity to become a teaching assistant. Teaching made me realize that understanding something yourself and explaining it clearly to someone else are two different skills. It changed how I communicate technical ideas.

During my master's, my work expanded across machine learning, generative AI, LLMs, RAG, and MLOps. Through projects in financial ML, GenAI, multimodal learning, and computer vision, I gradually became more interested in what happens around the model: the data, the systems, the connections between components, and what it takes to make something reliable enough to actually use.

That interest is what is pulling me deeper into generative AI, data engineering, cloud, and MLOps. I'm still building depth in these areas, but I enjoy learning by putting the pieces together, experimenting, and figuring out what breaks.

Looking back, Boston gave me more than a master's degree. It taught me that unfamiliar things eventually become things you know how to handle.

I am still learning, still breaking things, and still figuring out how to build them better. And honestly, that's probably the part of engineering I enjoy most.
