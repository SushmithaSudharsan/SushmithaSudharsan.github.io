ABOUT

I have always been curious about what makes things work.

Even before I knew the word debugging, I liked the process of figuring out why something wasn't behaving the way I expected. I would try something, change it, break it, and try again just to understand what was happening underneath.

That curiosity eventually found its way into programming.

As I moved into computer science and data analytics, I found that machine learning gave me a much bigger version of the same problem. A model might work well, but then the interesting questions begin: Is the data right? Why did the result change? What happens when the inputs change? How do you make the whole thing work outside a notebook?

My first deeper experience with that came through undergraduate research. I explored classroom engagement and fatigue using an RNN/LSTM model, which eventually became my first-author IEEE publication. The work achieved 96% accuracy across data from 30 participants, but what stayed with me most was the process of trying to find meaningful signal in real data.

That interest continued through my MS in Data Analytics Engineering at Northeastern University, where my work expanded into machine learning, generative AI, LLMs, Retrieval-Augmented Generation, and MLOps.

My projects have given me different ways to explore that curiosity. In my financial stress-testing project, I worked on the CI/CD workflow around multiple ML models and learned how experiment tracking, automated deployment, monitoring, and retraining become part of the system once a model moves beyond development.

With the GenAI sports analysis chatbot, I worked on the data pipeline and validation, FAISS vector store, and prompt templates. It showed me that building a useful AI application involves much more than connecting an LLM to a prompt. The data, retrieval, grounding, and surrounding engineering all matter.

I also explored multimodal learning through VisionScript, working with image-caption data and a BLIP-based captioning baseline as the project developed into a broader vision-language system.

My internship at Whirl Data Inc. gave me another perspective on working with real-world data. I worked on image-data quality checks that reduced annotation errors by 80% and contributed to improving a computer vision model's accuracy from 73% to 88%.

Across all of these experiences, I have gradually become more interested in what happens around the model: where the data comes from, how it changes, how different components connect, and what it takes to make a system reliable enough to actually use.

That is what pulled me beyond model building and into areas like generative AI, data engineering, cloud, and MLOps. I'm still building depth in these areas, but I enjoy learning them by putting the pieces together, experimenting, and figuring out what breaks.

I am still learning, still breaking things, and still figuring out how to build them better.

And honestly, that's probably the part of engineering I enjoy most.
