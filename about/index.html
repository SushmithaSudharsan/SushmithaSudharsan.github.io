ABOUT

I have always been curious about what makes things work.

Even before I knew the word debugging, I liked figuring out why something wasn't behaving the way I expected. I would try something, change it, break it, and try again just to understand what was happening underneath.

That curiosity led me to programming and then machine learning, where it turned into bigger questions: Is the data right? Why did the result change? How do you make something work beyond a notebook?

My first deeper experience with that came through undergraduate research on classroom engagement and fatigue using an RNN/LSTM model. It became my first-author IEEE publication in 2024, but what stayed with me most was learning how to find meaningful signal in real data.

Then came one of the biggest firsts in my life: moving to Boston for my MS in Data Analytics Engineering at Northeastern University, and living away from home for the first time. My first Boston winter is still one of my favorite memories. I saw snow for the first time, and by my second winter I was walking to class through it.

I learned to cook, manage my money and get around on the T, and living with people from different backgrounds taught me to adapt. When I'm not working, I'm usually exploring a new café.

As a graduate teaching assistant for Data Mining (IE 7275), I realized that understanding something yourself and explaining it clearly to someone else are two different skills. It changed how I communicate technical ideas.

One moment from my master's sums up what I enjoy. During a 48-hour hackathon, our World Cup chatbot gave answers that sounded reasonable even when the data behind them was incomplete. I traced each one step by step: what the user asked, what retrieval found, what reached the prompt, and how the model read it. That is the work I like most: the data, the systems and the connections around the model.

Boston gave me more than a master's degree. It taught me that unfamiliar things eventually become things you know how to handle.

I am still learning, still breaking things, and still figuring out how to build them better. And honestly, that's probably the part of engineering I enjoy most.
