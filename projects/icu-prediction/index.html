ARCHITECTURE

THE PROBLEM

How much can we predict from the first 24 hours of an ICU stay?

This project explored the use of machine learning to predict important ICU outcomes using information available during the first 24 hours of admission.

The project focused on two related problems: predicting in-hospital ICU mortality and predicting length of stay among patients who survived.

The underlying data came from MIMIC-IV, a large electronic health record dataset containing clinical information from ICU patients.

This created a practical machine learning challenge: the information was distributed across large clinical tables, contained missing values, and included many different types of measurements that needed to be prepared before modeling.

THE APPROACH

We treated mortality and length of stay as two different prediction problems.

The mortality task was formulated as a classification problem, while length of stay was treated as a regression problem among surviving patients.

01 · Mortality prediction

The objective was to predict in-hospital ICU mortality using clinical information available during the first 24 hours.

02 · Length of stay

Among surviving patients, the project examined ICU length of stay and the prediction of short stays of seven days or less.

The two tasks allowed us to compare different modeling approaches while working from the same early clinical window.

DATA PREPARATION

Most of the work started before the models were trained.

MIMIC-IV contains large clinical event tables, so some of the data had to be processed in chunks before it could be used for modeling.

Clinical, demographic, and diagnostic information was combined using patient and admission identifiers to create the modeling dataset.

The preprocessing workflow included filtering the study population, checking physiological plausibility, handling missing values through imputation, encoding categorical variables, and preparing the features for the two prediction tasks.

Building the modeling dataset

The goal was to create a consistent representation of the patient's early ICU state while avoiding information that would only become available later in the admission.

MODELING

Comparing different models rather than relying on one approach.

Mortality prediction

We compared Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost, and a Multi-Layer Perceptron for the mortality classification task.

Length of stay prediction

For length-of-stay prediction, we evaluated Elastic Net, KNN, Random Forest, and XGBoost among surviving patients.

Comparing several models made it possible to evaluate how different algorithms handled the same clinical feature space and prediction objectives.

RESULTS

Early clinical information showed measurable predictive signal.

For mortality prediction, XGBoost achieved an ROC-AUC of 0.90.

For length-of-stay prediction, the evaluated models produced MAE values between 0.94 and 0.96 days.

CHALLENGES

The hardest part was working with the structure of clinical data.

One of the main challenges was handling the scale and structure of the MIMIC-IV dataset. Clinical information was distributed across different tables and contained a large number of records.

Missing values were another important consideration. Clinical measurements are not necessarily recorded at the same frequency for every patient, so the preprocessing workflow had to account for incomplete observations.

These challenges reinforced the importance of understanding the data-generating process before choosing a modeling approach.

WHAT I LEARNED

In healthcare ML, the modeling is only one part of the problem.

Working with EHR data made the importance of preprocessing much more concrete for me. Large clinical datasets are not immediately ready for machine learning, and decisions around filtering, imputation, feature construction, and data selection can strongly influence the final results.

The project also gave me experience comparing classification and regression approaches while thinking about model performance within a specific application context.

More broadly, it reinforced an idea that has continued into my other ML work: understanding the data and the system around a model matters just as much as choosing the model itself.
